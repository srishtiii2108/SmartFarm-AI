# Tomato Plant Disease Identification and Integrated Management Guide
Source: SmartFarm Agronomic Extension Reference (Solanaceae Pathology Series)

## Tomato Early Blight (Alternaria solani)
### Symptoms and Identification
Tomato Early Blight is caused by the fungal pathogen Alternaria solani. Initial symptoms appear on older, lower leaves as small, dark brown to black circular lesions. As lesions expand, they develop characteristic concentric rings resembling a target board, often surrounded by a bright yellow chlorotic halo. Severe infection leads to premature defoliation, sunscald on exposed fruit, and reduced yield.

### Favorable Conditions and Prevention
Early blight thrives in warm temperatures (24°C to 29°C) combined with high relative humidity, heavy dew, or frequent rainfall. Preventive practices include practicing a 2-to-3-year crop rotation away from solanaceous crops (tomato, potato, pepper), staking or caging plants to improve canopy airflow, applying organic mulch to prevent soil splash onto lower foliage, and using drip irrigation instead of overhead sprinklers. Remove and destroy infected lower leaves early.

## Tomato Late Blight (Phytophthora infestans)
### Symptoms and Identification
Tomato Late Blight is a destructive oomycete disease caused by Phytophthora infestans. Symptoms begin as irregular, pale-to-dark green water-soaked spots on leaf tips and margins that rapidly turn dark brown or purplish-black. Under humid conditions, a white, fuzzy fungal-like growth appears on the underside of lesions. Stems may develop dark brown lesions, and green fruit can develop firm, greasy, dark brown blotches.

### Favorable Conditions and Prevention
Late blight spreads rapidly during cool, wet weather (15°C to 22°C with relative humidity above 85% or prolonged leaf wetness). Management relies on planting certified disease-free transplants, ensuring wide row spacing for rapid foliage drying, immediately removing infected volunteer plants or cull piles, and avoiding working in fields when leaves are wet.

## Tomato Bacterial Spot (Xanthomonas spp.)
### Symptoms and Identification
Bacterial Spot is caused by Xanthomonas species and affects leaves, stems, and fruit. Leaves develop small (1–3 mm), water-soaked, circular dark brown or black spots that may coalesce into large necrotic areas, causing leaves to turn yellow and drop. On green fruit, small raised black specks appear, eventually becoming sunken, scabby lesions.

### Favorable Conditions and Prevention
Warm temperatures (25°C to 30°C) and wind-driven rain or overhead irrigation promote rapid bacterial spread. Prevention includes using certified pathogen-free seed, hot-water seed treatment, avoiding overhead watering, sanitizing pruning tools between rows, and rotating out of tomatoes and peppers for at least two seasons.

## Tomato Leaf Mold (Passalora fulva)
### Symptoms and Identification
Tomato Leaf Mold is common in high-humidity greenhouses and polyhouses. Pale green or yellowish spots appear on the upper leaf surface, while the corresponding underside develops velvety olive-green to grayish-purple fungal sporulation. Heavily infected leaves curl, wither, and drop prematurely.

### Favorable Conditions and Prevention
Relative humidity above 85% and temperatures around 22°C to 25°C trigger outbreaks. Preventive cultural controls include increasing greenhouse ventilation, spacing plants adequately, pruning lower foliage to reduce canopy humidity, and watering early in the day so foliage dries before nightfall.

## Tomato Septoria Leaf Spot (Septoria lycopersici)
### Symptoms and Identification
Septoria Leaf Spot typically appears after the first fruit set on lower leaves. It produces numerous small (2–4 mm), circular spots with dark brown borders and tan or light gray centers containing tiny black fruiting bodies (pycnidia). Unlike Early Blight, Septoria lesions lack concentric target rings and rarely infect fruit directly.

### Favorable Conditions and Prevention
Warm, wet weather (20°C to 25°C) with splashing rain spreads spores from crop debris. Preventive measures include deep plowing or removing infected plant debris after harvest, mulching soil surfaces, eliminating weeds in the nightshade family, and maintaining drip irrigation.

## Tomato Yellow Leaf Curl Virus (TYLCV)
### Symptoms and Identification
TYLCV is a viral disease transmitted exclusively by the silverleaf whitefly (Bemisia tabaci). Infected plants exhibit severe stunting, upward curling and cupping of leaf margins, yellowing (chlorosis) between leaf veins, and flower drop resulting in drastic yield loss.

### Favorable Conditions and Prevention
Hot, dry weather favors whitefly population surges. Because viral infections cannot be cured once established in a plant, prevention focuses on vector exclusion: using fine insect-proof netting in nurseries, deploying yellow sticky traps for monitoring, planting TYLCV-resistant varieties, and promptly roguing (removing and bagging) symptomatic plants early.
